cofferdam

A write-ahead-logged key/value store, and a harness that kills it at every I/O boundary and checks that what recovery leaves behind is a state the API could actually have produced.

The method is bounded black-box crash testing, from ALICE/BOB (OSDI 2014) and CrashMonkey/ACE (OSDI 2018), which found 24 crash-consistency bugs in production Linux filesystems - one of them in a formally verified one. Their published finding, that such bugs overwhelmingly reproduce in three operations or fewer, is what makes a bounded space worth enumerating exhaustively instead of sampling.

The negative control

Without this, “it found bugs” is indistinguishable from “it fires at random”. The correct build never leaves more than two writes in flight at once, which is what a disciplined fsync buys: the reorder space stays small enough that the default bound of four enumerates all of it, so these crash points are proven rather than sampled.

The planted fixtures

What the reorder bound costs

Above the bound the schedule space is sampled, not enumerated, and every crash point whose sample found nothing is reported unverifiable. A corruption found under a sample is still definitive; an absence under a sample is not evidence of anything.

Three real targets

What this is not

  • Not a database. Single process, single writer, no concurrency model at all.
  • Not a replacement for SQLite or LevelDB, and not a competitor to them: one of them is a target here.
  • Not a filesystem checker. The device is modeled; its fidelity to NTFS is measured, not assumed.
  • Sub-sector tearing, bit rot, and a disk that lies about fsync are all real and all out of scope.
